# Ghost Recon Wildlands terrain: comparison and missing features

Written 2026-10-18. Source: "Ghost Recon Wildlands: Terrain Technology and
Tools", Guillaume Werle and Benoit Martinez, Ubisoft, GDC 2017 (104 slides
with speaker notes, `WERLE_MARTINEZ_GRWterrainTechnologyTools.pdf` in this
folder). Compared against:
- Terrain3D (`/Users/stefik/Repositories/Terrain3D`);
- what AllTerrainVehicle has now (Tiers 1 and 3, and the splatting
  prototype);
- the library changes already listed in `VulkanChanges.md`.

The goal stated by the user: terrain as robust as this "kind" of thing.
This file lists what that would take beyond what is already planned.

## What Ghost Recon Wildlands (GRW) built

Numbers are from the slides.

### Scale

- A 32k by 32k heightmap, with 4 height layers.
- 11 biomes, 143 terrain materials.
- 16 km² of rivers and lakes; 656 km of roads, 2150 km of footpaths; 90 km
  of railway.
- 58 generated villages.
- About 12 million instances: 840,000 trees, 3.4 million bushes, 775,000
  rocks.
- Up to 10 texels per cm and 1 triangle every 2 cm up close, consistent
  everywhere the player can go.

### Height and editing (slides 17-24)

- **Input:** a World Machine heightmap, 12 hours of computing on 16
  machines.
- **GPU sculpting:** areas up to 2 km by 2 km edited at once, at
  interactive frame rates, inside the game.
  - Brushes and heightmap are blended in a floating-point render target
    that the game shader reads directly.
  - "Stamp a whole mountain in one click", plus copy/paste and erase
    brushes.
- **Non-destructive height layers,** added together:
  - **Base:** the World Machine output.
  - **Macro:** large hand-sculpted features.
  - **DCC:** changes generated by the Houdini tools, such as roads and
    rivers carving the ground.
  - **Micro:** level design touch-ups.

  Erasing a layer returns to what was beneath it.

### Material distribution (slides 25-35)

- **Procedural rules, not hand painting:** painting the whole map by hand
  was ruled out early.
- **Rule conditions,** evaluated per pixel: slope, altitude, noise and
  curvature, each through a ramp, plus masks such as a flow map from World
  Machine.
- **Rule stacks.**
  - A "setting" (a biome) is a stack of rules, evaluated from the bottom
    up, and the last rule that succeeds picks the material.
  - Whole settings are painted, not single materials.
  - The rules also shape the brush's opacity ("procedural paint"), so
    painted transitions look natural.
- **Results are baked into two textures,** the same resolution as the
  heightmap:
  - a **splatting texture** (R8): one *material index* per sample, from up
    to 256 materials;
  - a **vista texture**: a ready-made color image of the terrain (like a
    satellite photo), used as the albedo for distant patches.
- **Storage:** everything is cut into tiles in a **quadtree**, used for
  levels of detail, culling and **streaming** on a loading thread.

### Rendering (slides 36-64)

- **PBR materials:** albedo, roughness, specular occlusion, normal, and
  **displacement** through tessellation ("next gen" look, at a large GPU
  cost).
- **143 materials can't all be loaded:**
  - the 32 nearest the player are cached in a texture array;
  - an indirection table maps a material index to its cache slot.
- **Foreground blending:**
  - the 4 neighboring splat samples, blended bilinearly;
  - slopes use **biplanar projection** to avoid stretching, which doubles
    the lookups.
  - The brute-force shader was too slow (12 materials). So each patch is
    split by a compute shader into flat, slope and transition parts, each
    with its own shader. 80% of the terrain then needs only 4 materials.
- **Roads:**
  - At first they were carved and splatted into the terrain. That gave
    poor transitions and little detail.
  - Screen-space decals were too slow, and didn't follow displacement.
- **Virtual texturing** solved both:
  - A feedback pass, recorded during the G-buffer pass, finds the visible
    tiles.
  - Tiles are generated on the fly, materials and decals blended, then
    compressed to BC formats in async compute.
  - Memory is 218 MB at 1080p and 1 GB at 4K, and drawing takes under
    8 ms on Xbox One.
  - It needed time-slicing to avoid hitches when driving fast.

### Procedural tools (slides 65-99)

- **Houdini:** about 145 tools, made by 1 technical director and 3
  technical artists, used by 30 world builders. Rule-based, deterministic,
  offline, run on the CPU and a render farm.
- **Tools read and write the terrain** by layer: base only, or all layers
  combined.
- **Derived terrain data,** recomputed automatically whenever the terrain
  changes:
  - roughness and ridges, from elevation;
  - wetness, from rivers and materials;
  - sunlight, averaged over the day;
  - water flow.
- **Layers built on the terrain.** Each takes explicit inputs (waypoints,
  curves) and implicit inputs (other layers' results):
  - **Roads:**
    - waypoints joined by **weighted anisotropic pathfinding** (Galin et
      al. 2010);
    - outputs: trajectories (reused for AI traffic), a terraforming height
      stamp, a splat mask, and bridges.
  - **Road props:** decals, crash barriers and power lines, placed
    automatically along road trajectories.
  - **Railways:** like roads, with gentler slopes, wider turns, tunnels and
    bridges, and crossing roads at right angles.
  - **Rivers and lakes:** curves become terraforming, splatting, a water
    surface mesh, a flow map in vertex colors, and a wetness mask.
  - **Settlements and fields:** whole villages placed from a center and a
    boundary. Outputs: terraforming, splatting, buildings, walls, extra
    paths.
  - **Rocks and vegetation:**
    - a distribution mask (material, occlusion, curvature, flow map,
      roughness);
    - distribution rules (models, alignment to slope, scale,
      density/minimum distance);
    - presets for most users.
  - **Sound:** ambient zones, wind and echo maps from terrain and
    vegetation.
- **Instancing:**
  - tools output only points (a matrix and an object ID), saved in a
    **points cache**;
  - a **subtractive workflow** checks every point against the terrain
    (snapped to the ground, keeping its offset, so rocks stay half buried)
    and against other layers (roads delete trees, rocks delete
    vegetation);
  - a **level-of-detail multiplier** per instance, balanced by local
    density.
- **Workflow:**
  - "always playable";
  - tile-based batch jobs, recomputed automatically when the terrain is
    submitted to version control;
  - several teams working on one world.

## Additional features needed (not in any plan yet)

Grouped by where the work goes. "Project" means it can be built in
AllTerrainVehicle first, with no library change. "Library" means it needs
changes to the renderer or language.

### Terrain data and tools (project)

1. **Non-destructive height layers.**
   - Several named height layers per region: base, macro, procedural,
     micro, or any list.
   - The terrain is their sum, and each can be erased or turned off.
   - Brushes write to the chosen layer. Generated features (roads, rivers)
     write to their own layer, so regenerating them doesn't wipe hand work.
   - `TerrainRegion` keeps one height array per layer plus the combined
     result.
   - This is the single most important workflow idea in the talk, and it
     fits our per-region files.
2. **Stamp, copy/paste and erase-layer brushes,** on top of the Tier 4
   brushes: placing a whole heightmap (a mountain) at once, and copying an
   area.
3. **Rule-based material distribution.**
   - A rule: a material plus conditions (slope range, altitude range,
     noise, curvature, flow), each through a ramp.
   - A setting (biome): a stack of rules, last success wins.
   - Painting settings by brush, with rules shaping the brush's opacity.
   - Deterministic and re-runnable after edits.
4. **Derived terrain maps,** computed from the heights and kept up to date
   per region:
   - curvature or roughness (ridges and gullies);
   - flow accumulation (where water runs);
   - sun exposure or occlusion;
   - wetness.

   They feed rule distribution, scattering, and sound.
5. **Splines and terraforming.**
   - Curves on the terrain that flatten or carve the ground along their
     width with falloff, written to a height layer.
   - Each also paints a splat mask, and keeps its trajectory for gameplay
     (AI, traffic).
   - Road placement by pathfinding: a weighted anisotropic shortest path
     over the heightmap, between waypoints.
   - Then rivers (carve plus a water surface), railways (gentler rules,
     tunnels, bridges as placed modules).
6. **Scatter rules and a points cache** (extends Tier 5):
   - distribution masks from the maps above, plus rules for model, slope
     alignment, scale, and density with minimum distance;
   - an exclusion pass against splines and other layers;
   - snapping to the ground that keeps each point's offset;
   - presets.
7. **Float heightmap import:** R16 (raw 16-bit) and R32 or EXR, like
   Terrain3D, for World Machine, Gaea and real elevation data.
8. **A baked far color ("vista") texture.** Blend the splat layers on the
   CPU into one color image for far models, so distant ground needs one
   texture lookup and matches near ground. With the coarse far models we
   already have, this is a natural next step.

### Terrain shader (project shader; library for a supported way to use it)

9. **Per-layer normal maps and roughness:** PBR terrain materials, which
   the deferred G-buffer already has room for.
10. **Biplanar or triplanar projection on slopes,** to stop stretching on
    cliffs. Terrain3D has a projection option too.
11. **A material index instead of four weights,** to reach many materials.
    Terrain3D's control map is the 32-material step; GRW's R8 index is the
    256-material step. Our splat texture would hold indices and blends.

### Library changes beyond `VulkanChanges.md` 1-20

12. **Many textures (texture arrays or a large bindless budget)** (extends
    #9, #19).
    - Fix the group size (#19).
    - Add 2D texture arrays, so dozens of material textures are one
      binding.
    - Add a way to swap textures in and out of an array, to cache nearby
      materials like GRW.
13. **Updating textures in place** (#20). Splat maps, derived maps and
    vista textures all change when the ground is edited.
14. **Tessellation pipelines.** The shader compiler already knows the
    stages, but the 3D painter has no tessellated pipeline. Needed for
    displacement. Large.
15. **Decals.** Projected textures onto the terrain (road markings, tire
    tracks), either as a G-buffer decal pass or baked into the splat or
    vista textures. Medium to large.
16. **Water rendering.** Transparent surfaces with a custom shader: flow
    map, depth color, reflections. Needs per-model shaders (#6), and a
    transparent pass that supports them. Large.
17. **Instancing at scale.**
    - Drawing a point cache (matrices plus model IDs) without an `Item3D`
      per instance, since `Layer3D` updates every item every frame.
    - Draw queues that grow (#18), culling per cell, and level of detail
      per instance.
    - Large; this is what millions of rocks and trees need.
18. **Background threads or async file loading** (language or library).
    Streaming today runs on the main thread, a few regions per frame. GRW
    streams on a loading thread, and virtual texturing time-slices across
    frames.
19. **Compute hooks and render-target feedback** (extends S2). GPU sculpting
    (blending brushes in a float render target), patch classification, and
    virtual texturing all need compute passes the project can add, and
    extra render targets in the G-buffer pass.
20. **Compressed textures (BC formats)** for memory. Needed at GRW's scale,
    not ours yet.
21. **Fog and atmosphere** (#17), and a **skybox** (#11). Every GRW vista
    relies on aerial perspective.

### Long term

22. **Virtual texturing:**
    - a feedback pass during the G-buffer, and a compute pass to list
      missing tiles;
    - tiles rendered and compressed on the fly;
    - a page table and an atlas.

    This is GRW's answer to blending cost and decals together. It depends
    on 13, 19 and 20, and threads (18).
23. **Gameplay layers from terrain:** sound zones, navigation (Terrain3D
    bakes navigation meshes), and road trajectories as AI paths.
24. **Tool workflow** (Quorum Studio):
    - deterministic, re-runnable tools, with presets;
    - automatic recompute of derived maps and layers when the terrain
      changes;
    - tile-based jobs.

    Our one-file-per-region format already suits version control and
    several people editing.

## What this changes in our priorities

1. **Library fundamentals come first,** since nearly everything rich
   depends on them:
   - #19 and #22 (texture groups), #6 (per-model shaders, **done**), #7
     (mipmaps; GRW's 10 texels per cm is meaningless without them), #20
     (texture updates);
   - then texture arrays (12).

   These are the same items the splatting prototype ran into.
2. **The biggest project-side wins, in order:**
   - height layers (1);
   - rule-based distribution with derived maps (3, 4);
   - splines with terraforming (5);
   - the vista texture (8).

   None needs a library change, and together they cover most of GRW's
   workflow ideas.
3. **Instancing at scale (17) is the largest library gap** for a world
   that looks like GRW's. It should be planned before Tier 5 starts.
4. **Tessellation, decals, water and virtual texturing** are separate
   large projects. Each should get its own investigation when its turn
   comes.

## Not taken from GRW

- Houdini and a render farm. Our tools would be Quorum code and Quorum
  Studio.
- Console-specific tuning (Xbox One numbers, BC compression on async
  compute).
- Assassin's Creed engine specifics.
